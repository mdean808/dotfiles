# Review

Review the draft as a separate read after you finish it. While you write,
you know what you meant, so you read that meaning into the words. The
review reads the words on the page and nothing else.

Read the whole draft once, then go one sentence at a time through checks
1 to 5. Finish with check 6, reading the whole draft as if you were
saying it out loud. Fix each problem by rewriting the sentence. Don't add
another sentence to explain the problem.

## 1. Every "it" points to one thing

For each "it", "this", "that", "they", "them" and "which", find the noun
the word stands for. Rewrite with the noun when:

- two nouns in the same or the previous sentence could be the one meant.
  "The app sends the file to the printer when it's ready." Which one is
  ready? Write "when the file is ready".
- the same pronoun means two things close together. "Drop the photo on
  the editor and it resizes it." Write "and the editor resizes the
  photo".
- the noun isn't in the text at all, and the reader has to guess it. A
  heading followed by "It runs every night" makes the reader look back
  for what "it" means. "A form that checks it" hides what gets checked,
  so write "a form for checking the address".

## 2. The small words are there

Read each sentence as normal speech. Put back any small word that was
dropped to save space: articles, prepositions and linking words.

- "Requires restart to apply" becomes "Restart the app to apply the
  change".
- "Sign in to see order" becomes "Sign in to see your order".
- "Saved, will sync later" becomes "Saved. Your changes will sync when
  you're back online."
- "Nothing stops the app overwriting your changes" becomes "Nothing stops
  the app from overwriting your changes".

Headings, buttons and labels are short on purpose, so they don't need
full grammar. Every sentence does, and in a long sentence a missing
"from", "to" or "the" is easy to miss.

## 3. Every line changes what the reader does

For each sentence, ask whether the reader would do anything differently
without it. If not, delete it. These lines usually fail:

- reassurance nobody asked for, such as "We take your privacy seriously"
  on a sign-up form.
- a fact that belongs in a different section, such as the licence in a
  "Usage" section.
- how something works, when the reader needs to know what happens.
  "Changes are queued and written in a batch", when "Changes save
  automatically" is all the reader needs.
- a line that restates the heading, the label or the previous sentence.
- a closing summary of what the text has said.

## 4. Nothing sounds final but says nothing

Some lines sound like a conclusion and give the reader nothing to use:
"Less is more", "The numbers speak for themselves", "That's the whole
idea". Look for a fact or an action in the line. If there isn't one,
delete the line. Otherwise write the fact or action as a plain sentence:
"Edits in the spreadsheet are overwritten at midnight."

## 5. Sentences are the right size

A sentence carries one main idea. Most run 8 to 20 words, and short
commands are fine.

- Split a sentence that stacks more than two of these: a "that" or
  "which" clause, a condition ("only if", "unless"), a comparison
  ("better", "more than"), or a list. "The tool is a scheduler for shared
  calendars that books a room only if everyone invited is free at a time
  that suits most of them" becomes "The tool books meeting rooms for
  shared calendars. It picks a time when everyone invited is free."
- Don't fix a long sentence by chopping it into fragments. "Fast. Simple.
  Free." is not three sentences.

## 6. The draft sounds like a person talking

Read the whole draft as speech.

- If a person wouldn't say a line to a colleague across a desk, rewrite
  it in the words they would use.
- Vary sentence length. Several sentences of the same length and shape
  in a row sound like a list read out.
- Don't repeat a word or construction close together. If "never",
  "only", "exactly" or a phrase like "X, not Y" comes up twice in a
  paragraph, rewrite one of them.
- Join related sentences with plain linking words such as "so", "but",
  "then" and "because". Don't line up bare statements one after another.
