# Review

Do this as a separate read after drafting. While writing you know what you
meant, so you read that meaning into the words. The review reads only what
is on the page.

Read the whole draft once. Then go one sentence at a time through checks 1
to 5. Then read the whole draft aloud for check 6. Fix each problem by
rewriting the sentence, not by adding a sentence to explain it.

## 1. Every "it" points to one thing

For each "it", "this", "that", "they", "them" and "which", find the noun it
stands for. Rewrite with the noun when:

- two nouns in the same or the previous sentence could be the one meant.
  "The app sends the file to the printer when it's ready." Which one is
  ready? Write "when the file is ready".
- the same pronoun means two things close together. "Drop the photo on the
  editor and it resizes it." Write "and the editor resizes the photo".
- the noun isn't in the text at all and the reader has to guess it from
  context. A heading followed by "It runs every night" makes the reader
  look back for "it".
- a clause ends in a pronoun that refers back to its own subject. "A
  form that checks it" hides what gets checked. Write "a form for checking
  the address".

## 2. The small words are there

Read each sentence as normal speech. Put back any small word that has been
dropped to save space: articles, prepositions and linking words.

- "Requires restart to apply" becomes "Restart the app to apply the
  change".
- "Sign in to see order" becomes "Sign in to see your order".
- "Saved, will sync later" becomes "Saved. It will sync when you're back
  online."
- "Nothing stops the app overwriting your changes" becomes "Nothing stops
  the app from overwriting your changes".

Headings, buttons and labels are short on purpose, so they don't need full
grammar. Every sentence does, including long ones, where a missing "from",
"to" or "the" is easy to miss.

## 3. Every line changes what the reader does

For each sentence, ask: would the reader do anything differently if this
were gone? If not, delete it. Lines that usually fail:

- reassurance nobody asked for, such as "We take your privacy seriously"
  on a sign-up form.
- a fact that belongs in a different section. A "Usage" section that
  mentions the licence.
- how it works, when the reader only needs what happens. "Changes are
  queued and written in a batch" when the reader only needs "Changes save
  automatically".
- restating the heading, the label or the previous sentence.
- a closing summary of what was just said.

## 4. Nothing sounds final but says nothing

Some lines sound like a conclusion and give the reader nothing to use:
"Less is more", "The numbers speak for themselves", "That's the whole
idea". Look for a fact or an action in the
line. If there isn't one, delete the line. If there is one, write the fact
on its own: "Edits in the spreadsheet are overwritten at midnight."

## 5. Sentences are the right size

A sentence carries one main idea, in 8 to 20 words.

- Split a sentence when it stacks more than two of these: a "that" or
  "which" clause, a condition ("only if", "unless"), a comparison
  ("better", "more than"), or a list. "The tool is a scheduler for shared
  calendars that books a room only if everyone invited is free at a time
  that suits most of them" becomes "The tool books meeting rooms for
  shared calendars. It picks a time when everyone invited is free."
- Don't fix a long sentence by chopping it into fragments. "Fast. Simple.
  Free." is not three sentences.
- A tagline or opening is 1 or 2 sentences.

## 6. It sounds like a person talking

Read the whole draft aloud.

- If a person wouldn't say it to a colleague across a desk, rewrite it in
  the words they would use.
- Vary sentence length. Several sentences of the same length and shape in
  a row sound like a list read out.
- Don't repeat the same word or construction close together. If "never",
  "only", "exactly" or a phrase like "X, not Y" comes up twice in a
  paragraph, rewrite one of them.
- Join related sentences with plain linking words such as "so", "but",
  "then" and "because" when they belong together. Don't line up bare
  statements one after another.
- Prefer the everyday verb: "mention" or "say", not "name" or "surface".
